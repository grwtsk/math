# Rational recovery of a divisor-sum recurrence

Authors: grwtsk contributors

Joseph M. Shunia, *Deterministic integer factorization from abundance decisions*, arXiv:2611.34242v1, Lemma 3.4, equations (3.7)–(3.11). The local namespace is `GRWTSK.IntegerFactorization.DivisorSumRecurrence`. The application is the factor-recovery step on a residual positive integer $w$ whose power divisor sums are already available. [Source, §3.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.34242v1#S3.SS3) · [Lean declarations](DivisorSumRecurrence.lean)

For a trial order $\ell$, write $p_i$ for the retained primes and $q_i=p_i^{v_{p_i}(w)}$ for the retained full prime powers. The retained moments are $m_j=\sum_i p_i^{-1}q_i^{-j}$. The integer polynomial is $P(T)=\prod_i(1-q_iT)=1+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}c_k T^k$. Its roots are the reciprocals. Substitution of each $q_i^{-1}$ in $P$ gives zero. Multiplication by $p_i^{-1}q_i^{-(J+s)}$ followed by summation gives the exact retained equations. In particular, the first column samples $J+s+1$. The right-hand side samples $J+s$.

The perturbation proof selects a coordinate of maximum absolute value. If $Ax=Ay$, the difference $v=x-y$ satisfies $Dv=v$. When $v$ is nonzero a strict row contraction contradicts maximality. For the solution error $v=A^{-1}b-c$, the identity $v=Dv+L(b-Ac)$ yields $\max|v_i|<1/2$. Every rational operation in these statements is exact. The reciprocal Vandermonde argument supplies a uniform bound $C$ for the actual inverse. The scaled integer window inequality supplies both rational error margins. These inputs enter the application theorem below. [Inverse row sums](ReciprocalVandermondeBound.lean) · [Window budget](DivisorSumRecurrenceError.lean)

For a one-component residual $w=4$, the actual sample is $\xi_j=(1/2)4^{-j}$. At $J=1$ the equation is $c_1/32=-1/8$, so $c_1=-4$. Using the same moment for the first column and right-hand side instead gives $-1$. The root of $P$ is $1/4$, while the root of the reversed monic polynomial is $4$. A perturbation reaching the boundary $+1/2$ can change the rounded integer. The one-component entries and the tie at one half are Lean regression examples. An exact-rational Gaussian elimination check with actual samples at $J=48$ gave $(-5,6)$ for $w=30$ retaining $2,3$, and $(-17,72)$ for $w=72$ retaining $8,9$. Both computations included all signed Euler terms. Across $2\le w\le64$ and all retained prefixes consisting of singletons below the first omitted node, exact Gaussian elimination at $J=96$ tested 101 systems. Every coordinate error was below $10^{-5}$.

The polynomial identity, the Vandermonde determinant and the rational elimination argument have mathematically different roles. ## From the window to a least component

For $2\le w\le n$, put $J=48(1+\lceil\log_2 n\rceil)^2$. The theorem `exists_recovered_root` provides an order $1\le\ell\le\lfloor\log_2 w\rfloor$ and a prime dividing $w$ whose full power is minimal and satisfies the rounded, reversed monic equation. It indexes the retained primes by a finite ordinal, identifies each full power by its singleton prime support, and bounds every retained prime and full power by the certified cutoff $B$. The coefficient mass satisfies $\prod_i(1+q_i)\le 2^\ell B^\ell\le n^3$. Matrix inversion and integer rounding in the conclusion operate on the normalized divisor sums of $w$. No chosen order, retained factor set, inverse-norm estimate or numerical margin is required from the caller. [Application theorem](DivisorSumRecoveredRoot.lean)
